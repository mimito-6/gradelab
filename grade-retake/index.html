How the grade retake math works

1. Replacement: swap the quality points, keep the credits

Your cumulative GPA is total quality points divided by total credits. Under grade replacement, the old grade's points come out and the new grade's points go in, over the same credit total: new GPA = (current GPA × earned credits − old points × course credits + new points × course credits) ÷ earned credits. At 3.00 over 30 credits, a 3-credit C to an A is (90 − 6 + 12) ÷ 30 = 3.20, a +0.20 lift.

2. One course is a small slice of the average

The lift shrinks as your transcript grows, because the retaken course is a smaller share of the total. The same 3-credit C to an A is worth +0.20 over 30 credits but only +0.07 over 90 credits ((270 − 6 + 12) ÷ 90 = 3.07). Even a dramatic rescue — a 4-credit F to a B at 2.80 over 60 credits — is only +0.20, landing at 3.00. Retaking pays off most early in your degree, in high-credit courses, with the biggest grade jump.

3. A retake can lower your GPA

Replacement cuts both ways: a worse second grade replaces the old one outright. Retaking a 3-credit B and earning a C at 3.00 over 30 credits gives (90 − 9 + 6) ÷ 30 = 2.90, a −0.10 drop — you spent a semester to go backward. Only retake a course you are confident you can beat, and check the scenario table above for the grade you would actually earn, not the grade you hope for.

4. Replace vs. average — the policy is worth more than the grade

Some schools do not replace the grade at all: both attempts count, so the new points are added on top and the credit total grows. The same 3-credit C to an A at 3.00 over 30 credits is worth +0.20 under replacement but only +0.09 under averaging ((90 + 12) ÷ 33 = 3.09). The policy switch above shows both numbers — check your school's catalog before you decide, because the policy changes the answer more than one letter grade does.

What this calculator cannot do

It cannot know your school's retake rules. Some schools cap the retake grade (often at a C), some only allow retaking D or F grades, some count both attempts but only the higher one toward the major, and some keep the old grade on the transcript even when it no longer counts toward the GPA. Weighted courses (honors/AP) use different point values — the weighted GPA calculator is the page for those. And a retake can affect financial aid (satisfactory academic progress counts attempted credits), so check with your advisor, not just this calculator.

Frequently asked questions

How much will retaking a class raise my GPA?

It depends on your current GPA, how many credits you have earned, and how many credits the course is worth. Under grade replacement: New GPA = (Current GPA × Earned Credits − Old Grade Points × Course Credits + New Grade Points × Course Credits) ÷ Earned Credits. For example, at 3.00 over 30 credits, retaking a 3-credit C and earning an A gives (90 − 6 + 12) ÷ 30 = 3.20 — a +0.20 lift. Enter your numbers in the calculator above for the exact answer.

Is it worth retaking a C?

It depends on how many credits you already have. The same retake — a 3-credit C to an A — is worth +0.20 at 3.00 over 30 credits, but only +0.07 over 90 credits, because each new grade is a smaller slice of a bigger average. Retaking a low grade in a high-credit course early in your degree pays off the most; retaking one course late in a long transcript barely moves the number.

What if my school averages both attempts instead of replacing the grade?

Then the same retake is worth much less. At 3.00 over 30 credits, retaking a 3-credit C and earning an A under averaging gives (90 + 12) ÷ 33 = 3.09 — only +0.09, less than half the +0.20 you get under replacement — because the old grade stays in the average and the credit total grows. Use the policy switch on the calculator to see both numbers, and check your school's catalog before you decide: the policy matters more than the grade.

Can retaking a class lower my GPA?

Yes — under grade replacement, a worse second grade replaces the old one outright. At 3.00 over 30 credits, retaking a 3-credit B and earning a C gives (90 − 9 + 6) ÷ 30 = 2.90, a −0.10 drop. Only retake a course you are confident you can beat; the calculator shows the outcome for every possible new grade so you can see the downside before you commit.

Does the retaken course add credits to my total?

Under grade replacement, no — you already earned those credits once, so the total stays the same and only the quality points change. Under grade averaging, yes — both attempts count, so the total grows by the course credits. That is why the same retake moves your GPA less under averaging: the improvement is spread over more credits.

How is this different from the GPA planner?

The GPA planner works forward: you enter a target cumulative GPA and it tells you what semester GPA you need next term. This retake calculator works backward on a grade you already have: it swaps one past grade for a new one and shows the exact new cumulative GPA — including the scenario table for every grade you might earn on the retake.
